# No App Verifies Its Treatment Doses

No competitor in the plant-disease-diagnosis market — global or Indian — publishes an independently verified accuracy figure for disease diagnosis specifically, nor cites a traceable institutional source for a single treatment dose, chemical or organic. **Plantix**, the category leader with 10 million-plus downloads, reports self-estimated "85–90%" accuracy with no independent field study behind it ([Harvard D³](https://d3.harvard.edu/platform-digit/submission/plantix-bringing-crop-science-machine-learning-to-millions/), [CGIAR](https://bigdata.cgiar.org/?p=650859)), and its organic-plus-chemical treatment messaging sits inside a commission-based business model that critics say incentivizes pesticide sales ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantix)). India's government and ICAR disease knowledge — the obvious raw material for anything more rigorous — exists almost entirely as unindexed PDFs scattered across dozens of institute websites, not as a queryable database. Traditional and Ayurvedic treatment knowledge is similarly fragmented: solid, dosed, peer-reviewed ICAR findings exist for specific biocontrol agents (Trichoderma, Pseudomonas fluorescens, neem seed kernel extract), but the headline traditional formulations popularized in agritech marketing — Kunapajala, Jeevamrutha, Panchagavya, Agniastra — rest on blog posts, preprints, and company marketing rather than primary agronomic verification, a pattern this report found repeated inside the user-supplied comparison document itself. The resulting market gap is not computer vision — CNN transfer learning on PlantVillage-style benchmarks is a solved, commoditized problem — it is **trustworthy integration**: no existing app pairs an evidence-graded treatment recommendation (organic or chemical) with an honest disclosure of how well-sourced that specific recommendation actually is. That gap is exactly what PDDS/HereForNature's existing `EvidenceStatus` and `ClaimEvidenceNote` architecture is built to fill, and the path forward is to expand crop-condition coverage using Andhra Pradesh's best-documented disease data, add a third-party vision API only as a non-authoritative triage aid, and treat every traditional-treatment addition with the same claim-level skepticism applied here to CIBRC doses.

## Plantix dominates mindshare, but no one has measured its disease accuracy independently

Globally, a 2022 peer-reviewed review of 606 app-store plant-disease apps narrowed the field to 17 for quality assessment and found **Plantix was the only one able to identify plants from images, detect diseases, and suggest treatment in one flow** ([Agronomy/DOAJ via arXiv:2208.02446](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.02446)). Plantix's own reported figures vary by source — "85 percent accuracy" per CGIAR, "upwards of 90%" per a Harvard case study, and over 90% (up to 95%+ on specific diseases) in a 2018 APS conference abstract — but **no independent, peer-reviewed field benchmark of Plantix exists in any source found**, a gap that persisted even through a 2020 CABI/PlantwisePlus project that collected 52,000 field images in Tamil Nadu to retrain the model without publishing before/after accuracy ([PlantwisePlus blog](https://blog.plantwise.org/2020/06/19/teaching-an-app-to-help-farmers/)). The user-supplied comparison document's claim that **Farmonaut achieves "a claimed 95% accuracy"** could not be corroborated by this research at all — Farmonaut does not appear anywhere in the independently researched source base, so that figure should be treated as an unverified marketing claim pending direct confirmation, not cited as fact.

The same evidentiary gap runs through general plant-ID apps once marketing claims meet independent testing. **PictureThis** advertises "98%+ accuracy," and the comparison document goes further, stating it "correctly identified up to 100% of certain species" in "controlled trials." Independent academic testing tells a materially different story: a University of Wisconsin DNA-barcoding study found only **51% of PictureThis IDs matched genetic species identification, with 23% showing no correspondence at all** ([minds.wisconsin.edu](https://minds.wisconsin.edu/handle/1793/97276)); a peer-reviewed toxic-plant study found it correct on 10 of 17 species (59%) ([PMC8938528](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8938528)); and University of Maryland Extension's nine-app pasture-plant test put it at 94% first-suggestion accuracy ([UMD Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/snap-it-or-scrap-it-putting-plant-id-apps-test)) — the single highest independent figure found, but still short of "100%," and for species identification, not disease diagnosis. The comparison document's parallel claim that **PlantIn achieves "a perfect 100% accuracy score"** could not be corroborated by any source in this research pass — PlantIn simply does not appear in the independently gathered literature, so this is a second claim that should be flagged, not repeated, in any report this app publishes. Two patterns emerge consistently: accuracy figures that survive marketing copy collapse under independent testing, and the independent tests that do exist almost always measure species identification rather than disease diagnosis, meaning even a "verified" plant-ID accuracy number says little about diagnostic reliability.

On the Indian side, the competitive field bifurcates into three types: pure-play AI diagnosis apps (Plantix), agri-commerce platforms that bolt diagnosis onto input sales (AgroStar, which claims 10 million-plus monthly active users and pairs photo upload with a call-center "agri doctor" — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2019/03/13/agrostar-gets-27m-series-c-to-give-more-indian-farmers-increase-crop-production-with-data-analytics/)), and government/CSR advisory tools that blend static content with human expert review (Kisan Suvidha, ITC's Crop Doctor inside ITCMAARS, Fasal). None of these publish any accuracy figure at all — company or independent — for their diagnostic or advisory output. Andhra Pradesh and Telangana are unusually active testbeds for this category: Plantix launched its first regional-language (Telugu) version in AP in 2017, AP's Department of Agriculture signed an AI-agriculture MoU with Wadhwani AI in February 2026, and neighboring Telangana's Digital Green-run "Saagu Baagu" chilli pilot reported a WEF-documented **21% yield increase and 9% pesticide-use reduction** ([WEF](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2024/01/ai-for-agriculture-in-india/)) — but that figure describes a broader WhatsApp-chatbot advisory program, not a standalone image-diagnosis tool, and should never be cited as a disease-detection accuracy number. No commercial competitor was found to publish AP-specific crop-model tuning, which is itself a gap PDDS is positioned to close.

## A small team's realistic stack is a fine-tuned CNN, not a black-box API

The technology to build a competent disease classifier is commoditized and does not require a large team. **PlantVillage** remains the dominant public training set — 54,306 images across 38 classes/14 species, the basis of the foundational Mohanty, Hughes & Salathé 2016 paper that reported **99.35% in-distribution accuracy collapsing to 31.4% on independently sourced online images** ([arXiv:1604.03169](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.03169)) — and that in-distribution/out-of-distribution gap is the single most important caveat for any accuracy figure cited anywhere in this domain, including the comparison document's own undifferentiated "98% to 99%" claim for EfficientNet/MobileNet, which does not specify whether it reflects lab or field conditions. **PlantDoc**, built at IIT Gandhinagar explicitly around India's cited 35% annual crop-loss-to-disease statistic, is the most India-relevant alternative — non-lab, internet-scraped images across 13 species, with the authors reporting up to a 31% accuracy gain when models are exposed to field-realistic images rather than lab-only data ([arXiv:1911.10317](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.10317)) — though at roughly 2,600 images it is small and would need combining with PlantVillage or fresh field collection for production use. No large, standardized, multi-crop Indian dataset comparable in scale to PlantVillage was found; India-specific sets like the Indian Rice Disease Dataset are narrow, single-crop efforts ([IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/comment/6166)).

On architecture, CNN transfer learning (MobileNet, EfficientNet, ResNet) remains dominant and is the lower-risk choice for a small team, since published work consistently shows fine-tuning pretrained weights beats training any architecture — CNN or Vision Transformer — from scratch, where accuracy drops to the 84–90% range even on comparable tasks ([Atlantis Press ICETTACS](https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/icettacs-26/126027782)). MobileNet/EfficientNet-B0-class models are repeatedly favored for exactly the reason that matters for a rural-facing app: they are lightweight enough for on-device, offline inference. Multiple 2025–2026 papers caution that PlantVillage- and Plant-Pathology-2021-style benchmark accuracy is now "largely considered solved" and a poor predictor of field performance ([arXiv:2605.08618](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08618)), reinforcing that a team should validate on field-realistic imagery (PlantDoc, or its own collected photos) before trusting any benchmark number.

For a third-party API, the realistic choice set is narrow. **Pl@ntNet** offers 500 free species identifications/day but does no disease detection at all ([Pl@ntNet quota docs](https://my.plantnet.org/doc/api/quota)). **Kindwise** (Plant.id / plant.health / crop.health) is the only vendor whose API explicitly claims disease detection, on a shared credit system starting with 100 free credits and falling from roughly €0.05 to €0.01 per identification at volume ([Kindwise pricing](https://kindwise.com/pricing)) — broadly consistent with the detailed tiered pricing table in the comparison document, though this research could not independently verify Plant.id's exact current credit-per-call cost separately from Kindwise's general pricing page, so the document's specific tier figures should be re-confirmed directly before being relied on for a budget. Kindwise's own disclosed disease-specific accuracy is a 2022 figure of 55.6%→61.2% top-3 accuracy ([Kindwise](https://www.kindwise.com/post/health-assessment-production-release)) — now several years stale and never independently benchmarked — though its 2026-dated expansion to 548 disease classes (up from 90) is independently corroborated across two separate research-note passes and is one of the few specific figures in the comparison document that holds up. **Google Cloud Vision has no plant-disease or plant-ID feature at all** and should not be considered. No production case study was found of any competitor using a general-purpose multimodal LLM (GPT-4o, Gemini, Claude) as its primary diagnosis engine, nor could this research confirm the comparison document's claims about YOLOv8/v11 lesion-localization or the specific 2025/2026 free-vision-model roster on OpenRouter — both should be treated as directionally plausible but unverified until checked at decision time, since model/pricing pages change quickly and this research's own fetch tools could not reach several of the relevant domains.

## Government disease data is real, authoritative, and almost entirely unstructured

Every Indian institutional source this research examined — ICAR, ANGRAU, CIBRC, NBAIR, AIKosh — confirms real, citable disease and pest knowledge, but confirms it in a form a small team cannot query programmatically. ICAR's knowledge is spread across dozens of institute-specific technical bulletins on the **KRISHI repository** (krishi.icar.gov.in) and individual institute domains (CICR for cotton, IIMR for maize, NRCB for banana, CCARI for turmeric), each with its own publication convention and none offering a REST API or structured export — the realistic ingestion pattern is PDF scraping institute-by-institute, not a unified pipeline. ANGRAU's flagship AP-specific resource, the annual **Vyavasaya Panchangam** almanac, is a paid physical book (~Rs. 310) with no freely downloadable or digitized version located in this research, meaning its disease-and-pest content cannot currently be scraped at all without physically acquiring a copy. **CIBRC's "Major Uses of Pesticides" registry**, the nearest thing to an official chemical-dose reference, is confirmed to be a set of static, periodically re-uploaded PDFs at ppqs.gov.in with no search interface, API, or CSV export ([ppqs.gov.in](https://ppqs.gov.in/sites/default/files/major_uses_of_pesticides_insecticides_bio_insecticides_as_on_31.03.2024.pdf)) — this directly contradicts the comparison document's implication that the app's backend could "dynamically map its diagnostic outputs" to a live CIBRC source; in reality any such mapping requires manual or scripted PDF table-extraction against a dated snapshot, refreshed on no fixed schedule. The most structurally promising government-linked open dataset found is **AIKosh's pest-surveillance dataset** (aikosh.indiaai.gov.in), with explicit state/district/crop/pest/image fields, though its AP coverage and completeness were not independently verified in this pass.

Within that fragmented landscape, Andhra Pradesh-specific severity data does exist for several of PDDS's current ten crops and supports clear prioritization for new crop-conditions. **Groundnut, chilli, cotton, and turmeric have the strongest AP-district-level quantitative data**: a 2024 Nellore district trial measured groundnut rust at 16% in farmer practice versus 5% in fungicide-treated demonstration plots ([oar.icrisat.org](https://oar.icrisat.org/411/1/58373.pdf)); a Krishna district chilli study found thrips-driven "murda disease" reduced from 16.0% to 9.5% under IPM ([phytojournal.com](https://www.phytojournal.com/archives/2019/vol8issue4/PartAY/8-4-584-112.pdf)); a 2023-24 ANGRAU survey in Nandyal/Kurnool recorded cotton whitefly crossing economic threshold levels alongside pink bollworm ([sasapjas.org](https://sasapjas.org/?p=5691)); and an ICAR-associated survey found turmeric rhizome rot incidence peaking at **32.22% in Guntur district** specifically ([ccari.icar.gov.in](https://ccari.icar.gov.in/dss/turmaric.html)). Rice sheath blight has a dedicated AP severity survey (Nellore/Chittoor, 6.11–13.44%) and maize Turcicum leaf blight has an explicit ICAR-IIMR dosing recommendation (Mancozeb 2.5 g/l, [aicrpmaize.icar.gov.in](https://aicrpmaize.icar.gov.in/?p=1163)). By contrast, **sugarcane, mango, banana, and tomato have the weakest AP-specific sourcing** found in this research — their best-documented additional conditions (banana Sigatoka leaf spot, mango malformation, tomato leaf curl virus) rely on national or other-state ICAR data rather than confirmed Andhra Pradesh incidence figures, a distinction that should carry through directly into how confidently any new entry for these crops is labeled.

## Traditional-treatment doses are the least verified claims in this entire landscape — including in the comparison document itself

Three official Indian bodies genuinely document organic and biological crop protection: the **National Centre for Organic and Natural Farming (NCONF)**, ICAR-IIFSR's **All India Network Programme on Organic Farming**, and **NIPHM Hyderabad**, which holds statutory Central Insecticide Laboratory authority for biopesticide testing under Section 16 of the Insecticides Act, 1968 ([niphm.gov.in](https://niphm.gov.in/Training/qcmb26.pdf)). Genuinely dosed, peer-reviewed findings exist inside ICAR's own journal repository, epubs.icar.org.in: *Pseudomonas fluorescens* at a 5% solution (2×10⁹ cfu) controlling rice sheath blight in a Tripura trial ([epubs.icar.org.in](https://epubs.icar.org.in/index.php/IPPJ/article/view/28116)); and — notably — a Rajasthan groundnut trial in which *Trichoderma viride* seed treatment plus two foliar sprays of **5% neem seed kernel extract (NSKE)** reduced early leaf spot by **60.54%** relative to untreated control ([epubs.icar.org.in](https://epubs.icar.org.in/index.php/IJAgS/article/download/87012/35570/222786)). That NSKE figure independently corroborates the comparison document's description of NSKE as an ICAR-recognized botanical pesticide, though its further claim that NSKE is "effective against over 250 species of agricultural pests" was not independently found anywhere in this research and should be treated as unconfirmed breadth, separate from the verified 5%-dose finding. The regulatory DPPQS "Major Uses of Pesticides — Bio-Insecticides" PDF (ppqs.gov.in) is a real, periodically updated official document with per-crop dose and dilution fields, but its specific contents could not be directly fetched and confirmed in this research pass — any dose attributed to it should stay provisional pending a direct read.

Against that backdrop, the comparison document's four headline traditional formulations — **Kunapajala, Jeevamrutha, Panchagavya, and Agniastra**, each presented with specific quantities (e.g., "50g tobacco, 50g green chillies, 50g garlic, 500g neem leaves in 1,500mL cow urine" for Agniastra, and precise pH/phosphorus/potassium trajectories for Kunapajala fermentation) — could not be corroborated by this research's independently gathered primary sources at all; none of NCONF, ICAR-IIFSR, NIPHM, or epubs.icar.org.in material surfaced any of these four names or doses. Examining the comparison document's own works-cited list clarifies why its evidentiary footing varies by formulation. **Agniastra's sole citation is a gardening WordPress blog** ("How to Prepare Agniastra – Organic Pest-Control Techniques," theweekendagriculturist.wordpress.com) — a non-institutional source, meaning its exact dose figures have no verifiable agronomic backing at all. **Kunapajala and Panchagavya**, by contrast, are cited in that same document to a mix of sources of genuinely varying quality: a ResearchGate preprint and a Scribd upload (both non-peer-reviewed), but also an article in *PMC* (PubMed Central, PMC5884015, "Nutrient analysis of Kunapajala and Pancha gavya") and one in *Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems* (DOI 10.3389/fsufs.2022.1073010) — both indexed, ostensibly peer-reviewed venues. This research did not independently retrieve or verify either article's actual content, so neither the specific pH trajectory (6.74 → 3.47 → 8.81 over 40 days) nor the phosphorus/potassium figures the comparison document attributes to them can be called confirmed here — but they are meaningfully better-footed candidates for follow-up verification than Agniastra's blog-only sourcing, and should be prioritized for a direct primary-source read before any decision on inclusion. **TKDL**, often assumed to be the natural home for this kind of traditional knowledge, is not a usable source at all for crop-protection content: its established, decades-old scope is strictly medicinal (Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, Sowa Rigpa formulations for human health), it is available only by paid subscription to patent offices, and a December 2025 academic paper describing a CSIR effort to extend it to agricultural practices is a research proposal, not a live public database ([or.niscpr.res.in](https://or.niscpr.res.in/index.php/ALIS/article/view/25672)).

## The gap is not accuracy, it is honesty about accuracy

Every thread in this research converges on the same structural finding: the plant-disease-diagnosis market has plenty of confidence and almost no verification. Companies self-report accuracy against undisclosed test sets; independent studies, when they exist, test a different capability (species ID, not disease) or return substantially lower numbers; government disease data is real but trapped in unindexed PDFs; and the traditional-treatment content now circulating in agritech marketing — including in a document produced specifically to inform this app's strategy — passes through the same blog-and-preprint pipeline the app's own evidence-integrity rules were built to catch. No competitor found in this research publishes a treatment dose, chemical or organic, traced to a specific institutional page, table, or label section — Plantix markets "both chemical and organic solutions" while facing documented criticism that its commission-based model may favor pesticide sales ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plantix)), and every other Indian competitor examined discloses no treatment-sourcing methodology at all. A small team cannot out-spend Plantix's 120-million-image training set or Farmonaut's satellite infrastructure. It can out-verify them, by doing exactly what this cross-check exercise did: grading every claim by its actual source, not its confidence.

## Recommendations for the PDDS/HereForNature codebase

**Crop-conditions to prioritize adding, in order, and why:** Lead with **groundnut rust** (pairs with the existing Tikka entry, backed by a specific Nellore-district fungicide-trial figure — 16% vs. 5% — [oar.icrisat.org](https://oar.icrisat.org/411/1/58373.pdf)), **chilli thrips/murda disease** (Krishna-district IPM data showing 16.0%→9.5% reduction, [phytojournal.com](https://www.phytojournal.com/archives/2019/vol8issue4/PartAY/8-4-584-112.pdf)), **cotton whitefly** (Nandyal/Kurnool ETL-crossing survey data, [sasapjas.org](https://sasapjas.org/?p=5691)), and **turmeric leaf spot** (*Colletotrichum capsici*, pairs with the existing rhizome-rot entry which already has the strongest AP figure found in this research, Guntur 32.22%). These four crops have the best AP-district-level quantitative backing of any in the current catalog and should justify `evidenceStatus: 'provisional'` or better from day one. Next tier: **rice sheath blight** (AP-specific Nellore/Chittoor severity survey) and **maize Turcicum leaf blight** (an actual ICAR-IIMR institutional dose recommendation — Mancozeb 2.5 g/l — exists and should be entered with a `ClaimEvidenceNote` once the source page is directly confirmed). Lowest priority, and only with `evidenceStatus: 'provisional'` or `'unverified'` rather than `'verified_reference'`: new conditions for **sugarcane, mango, banana, and tomato**, since this research found their best-available additional diseases (fall armyworm, mango malformation, banana Sigatoka leaf spot, tomato leaf curl virus) rest on national or other-state data, not confirmed AP incidence — the catalog should say so explicitly rather than implying AP-specific validation it doesn't have.

**Third-party API: integrate narrowly, later, and never as the source of truth.** Of the three realistic options, Pl@ntNet is species-ID only and irrelevant to disease diagnosis; Google Cloud Vision has no plant-disease feature at all and should be ruled out entirely. **Kindwise (Plant.id/plant.health/crop.health)** is the one API worth prototyping now, but only inside its 100-free-credit trial, and only as a non-authoritative triage layer that helps route an uploaded photo toward a candidate crop-condition in PDDS's own catalog — never as the source of a displayed treatment or dose. Its own most recent disclosed disease-specific accuracy (55.6%→61.2% top-3, 2022) is stale and has never been independently benchmarked, so any Kindwise-sourced suggestion should be modeled the same way the codebase already models a `ClaimVerificationStatus: 'unsupported'` claim: shown, but explicitly and visibly caveated, never treated as equivalent to a catalog-verified diagnosis. Defer any paid Kindwise tier until the team has measured its accuracy directly against PDDS's own AP crop-condition photos; defer any vision-LLM integration indefinitely, since no production precedent for this use case was found anywhere in this research.

**Traditional-treatment sources: what's solid enough to add now, with what status.** Safe to add as `provisional`, moving toward `verified_reference` once a `ClaimEvidenceNote.locator` is filled in from a direct read of the primary document: **NSKE at 5% concentration** for leaf-spot-type fungal conditions (ICAR/epubs.icar.org.in, Rajasthan groundnut trial, 60.54% reduction — note the trial is not AP-specific, so the `ClaimEvidenceNote` should say so), and **Trichoderma/Pseudomonas fluorescens biocontrol doses** already published on epubs.icar.org.in for rice sheath blight and nematode management. The DPPQS bio-insecticide/bio-fungicide PDFs (ppqs.gov.in) are the right class of source for chemical-dose verification but must stay `unverified` or `provisional` until someone actually opens the PDF and extracts a page/table locator — this research could not fetch them directly, and neither should the app claim it has. **Do not add Agniastra in its currently circulating recipe form** (50g tobacco/chilli/garlic, 500g neem, 1,500mL cow urine): its only identified source anywhere, including in the comparison document that popularized it, is a gardening blog, which should map directly to `ClaimVerificationStatus: 'unsupported'` if it is entered at all — and per the app's existing rule, an unsupported dose should be suppressed or paired with an unmistakable verification disclosure, not displayed as actionable guidance. **Kunapajala, Jeevamrutha, and Panchagavya** are worth a dedicated follow-up research pass specifically to pull the PMC5884015 and Frontiers (10.3389/fsufs.2022.1073010) articles directly — they are plausible peer-reviewed anchors this research could not independently confirm — but until that follow-up happens, any entry using them should carry `evidenceStatus: 'unverified'` at best, with their specific numeric claims (the pH/phosphorus/potassium trajectory, the fermentation-day thresholds) flagged individually via `ClaimEvidenceNote` as `'unverified'`, not folded silently into a general organic-treatment description. **Do not cite TKDL** for any crop-protection content; its confirmed scope is medicinal only, and its agricultural expansion is a still-unpublished research proposal.

**Schema and architecture changes to support expansion.** The existing `EvidenceStatus`/`ClaimEvidenceNote` split is already the right shape for everything above and needs no redesign, but two additions would materially help as the catalog grows past ten crops. First, add a `geographicScope` field (e.g., `'ap_specific' | 'other_state' | 'national' | 'international'`) to `KnowledgeSource`, since this research repeatedly found the same institutional source (ICAR, epubs.icar.org.in) producing dosed findings from Tripura, Rajasthan, or Tamil Nadu trials that are credible in general but not AP-validated — the current schema has no way to say "this is a real ICAR citation, but not an AP one," which is exactly the distinction several findings above require. Second, given how often a third-party API (Kindwise or any future one) will be the trigger for surfacing a candidate disease before the catalog's own evidence exists for it, add an explicit `suggestionSource: 'catalog' | 'third_party_api_unverified'` flag at the diagnosis-result level, so the UI can structurally guarantee that any API-only suggestion without a matching catalog entry is rendered with its own distinct, unsuppressable low-confidence disclosure — never allowed to inherit the visual trust of a fully-sourced catalog entry. Both changes are additive to the existing types in `src/lib/knowledge/types.ts` and require no migration of current entries, since every field involved is optional.
