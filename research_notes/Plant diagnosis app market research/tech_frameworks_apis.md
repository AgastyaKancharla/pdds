# Tech Frameworks, APIs & Datasets for Plant Disease/Species Visual Analysis

## What third-party plant-ID/plant-health APIs exist, what do they cost, and what do they detect?

### Takeaway
A small team has three realistic third-party API families to choose from: Kindwise's product suite (Plant.id, crop.health, plant.health, insect.id, etc., all on one shared credit system with a 100-credit free trial), Pl@ntNet's API (species ID only, free up to 500 ID/day), and consumer apps like Plantix (no public developer API, closed platform) used as a competitive/UX reference rather than an integration option. Google Cloud Vision does not offer a dedicated plant-ID or plant-disease feature — only generic label detection that may incidentally return plant-related labels.

### Cited Findings
- Kindwise (the company behind Plant.id, plant.health, crop.health, mushroom.id, insect.id) runs a single credit-based pricing system across its products: new accounts get 100 free credits, each identification call costs 1 credit, and purchased credit price falls with volume from €0.05/credit (1,000 credits) down to €0.01/credit (1.5 million credits); purchased credits are valid for 3 months (not applicable to purchases under 30,000 credits) — [Kindwise Pricing](https://kindwise.com/pricing)
- Kindwise also offers retroactive/pay-as-you-go billing (pay only for credits actually used, invoiced monthly) as an alternative to prepaid credit blocks — [Kindwise FAQ](https://www.kindwise.com/faq)
- Kindwise's plant.health product distinguishes a "healthy" result (1 credit) from a "diseased" result that includes a health/disease assessment (2 credits), and lists a roughly 90-class disease identification set — [Kindwise plant.health](https://www.kindwise.com/plant-health)
- Kindwise offers discounted pricing for identifying a narrower class set (e.g., invasive plants only, medicinal mushrooms only) and custom/NGO pricing for qualifying non-profits — [Kindwise Pricing](https://kindwise.com/pricing)
- Plant.id (Kindwise's flagship identification product) is positioned as covering a very broad species catalog for identification, as contrasted against Plantix (disease-focused) in Plantix's own comparison page — [Plantix: compare plant id](https://plantix.net/en/plantix-intelligence/api-toolkit/compare-plant-id/)
- Exact Plant.id-specific monthly/subscription plan pricing (as opposed to the shared Kindwise credit system) could not be located in this research pass; Kindwise's pricing/FAQ pages describe the shared credit mechanics but not a Plant.id-specific plan table — **flagged as unverified, see Gaps**
- Pl@ntNet's official API free tier is 500 identifications/day per user/app, with quotas reset at 00:00:00 UTC — [Pl@ntNet Quota management](https://my.plantnet.org/doc/api/quota)
- Pl@ntNet's paid "Pro" tier is billed on a yearly contract starting at a 200,000-identification block, with per-1,000-ID rates falling as volume grows (the pricing page cites ~€5/1,000 IDs at the 200k+ tier, down to ~€2/1,000 IDs at 300M+); the Pro-plan documentation separately describes an initial €1,000 payment covering 200,000 requests with metered billing beyond that — sources differ slightly on the exact mechanics, so the current contract terms should be checked directly — [Pl@ntNet Pricing](https://my.plantnet.org/pricing); [Pl@ntNet Pro plan docs](https://my.plantnet.org/doc/getting-started/pro-plan)
- Pl@ntNet offers free access for qualifying non-profit educational/scientific use on request, and a listed €1,000/year option in that category; it also requires attribution for free-tier use and disallows multiple free accounts per person/organization — [Pl@ntNet Terms of use](https://my.plantnet.org/terms_of_use); [Pl@ntNet Pricing](https://my.plantnet.org/pricing)
- Pl@ntNet's API is species-identification only (no disease/health detection claim found in any source checked).
- Google Cloud Vision API's documented capabilities are general image labeling, face/landmark detection, OCR, and explicit-content tagging — no dedicated plant species or plant-disease detection feature appears in its documentation; it could return generic plant-related labels via its general label detector but with no botanical taxonomy or health-assessment claim — [Cloud Vision label detection docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vision/docs/quickstart-client-libraries)
- Plantix (by Peat GmbH, acquired by Germany's Helm AG in 2023) is a farmer-facing app, not a published developer API in the sources checked; it claims to use deep learning against an internally curated database and reports conflicting scale figures across sources: "800 symptoms across 60 crop types" in one source vs. "45–82 crops" and "500–780+ diseases/symptoms" in others, and a self-reported "90% accuracy" figure that should be treated cautiously as it is not independently verified — [GSMA: Plantix AI insights](https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-for-development/programme/agritech/detecting-and-managing-crop-pests-and-diseases-with-ai-insights-from-plantix/); [bestpractice.ai Plantix case study](https://www.bestpractice.ai/ai-case-study-best-practice/peat_identifies_400_diseases_and_pests_from_farmers'_uploaded_photos_on_their_new_mobile_app_)
- Plantix reports a training database of "over 120 million images of symptomatic crops, contributed by farmers worldwide" and localizes treatment recommendations using ICRISAT (International Crops Research Institute for the Semi-Arid Tropics) agronomic data — [GSMA: Plantix AI insights](https://www.gsma.com/solutions-and-impact/connectivity-for-good/mobile-for-development/programme/agritech/detecting-and-managing-crop-pests-and-diseases-with-ai-insights-from-plantix/)
- PlantSnap (a separate consumer plant-ID app, built with Imagga's custom-trained classifier) is reported at "90% precision rate for top-5 results" per Imagga's own case study — a vendor-reported figure, not independently benchmarked — [Imagga PlantSnap case study](https://imagga.com/success-stories/plantsnap-case-study)

### Inferences
- For a no/minimal-budget student team, Pl@ntNet's 500 free IDs/day is likely the most generous no-cost ceiling for species-only identification, while Kindwise's 100-free-credit trial is better suited to prototyping/testing a disease-detection flow before needing to pay (at ~€0.01–0.05/credit beyond the trial, cost is low at small scale but is a recurring marginal cost, unlike a one-time open-source model deployment).
- Because Pl@ntNet is species-ID only and Kindwise's plant.health/crop.health products are the ones that claim disease detection, a disease-diagnosis app would likely need Kindwise (or an in-house trained model) rather than Pl@ntNet alone.
- Google Cloud Vision is not a viable drop-in for plant disease or even species identification; it would only be useful as a generic pre-filter (e.g., confirming "this image contains a plant/leaf").

### Gaps

The exact Plant.id-specific credit cost per identification call (vs. the general Kindwise credit price table) is unconfirmed.
- Could not independently verify Plantix's current (2026) accuracy, crop-count, or disease-count figures — sources conflict and some are evidently outdated or marketing-derived; no primary/technical paper from Plantix/Peat/Helm was found.
- No confirmation found of a "PictureThis API" being publicly available for third-party integration (PictureThis is referenced in the task prompt but did not surface in search results as having a public developer API) — flagged as unconfirmed/likely not offered.
- WebFetch tool calls to openrouter.ai and web.plant.id failed with DNS resolution errors during this research session (environment/network issue), so direct pricing-page fetches could not be completed; findings above rely on WebSearch snippets only.

## What publicly available, commonly-used training datasets exist for crop disease image classification?

### Takeaway
The field's dominant training set is PlantVillage (and its Kaggle-hosted augmented derivative, "New Plant Diseases Dataset," ~87,000 images / 38 classes), but it is a lab-background dataset that does not generalize well to field images; PlantDoc (built by an Indian team at IIT Gandhinagar, explicitly framed around India's crop-loss problem) and the Plant Pathology 2020/2021 (FGVC) Kaggle competitions are the next most commonly cited alternatives, each covering different gaps (real-world/in-field images, and fine-grained apple foliar disease, respectively).

### Cited Findings
- The original PlantVillage-based study (Mohanty, Hughes & Salathé, 2016, Frontiers in Plant Science) trained a CNN on 54,306 images of diseased and healthy leaves spanning 14 crop species and 26 diseases (or healthy), reaching 99.35% accuracy on a held-out test split from the same dataset — [arXiv:1604.03169](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.03169); [Frontiers in Plant Science](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2016.01419/text)
- The same paper reports a dramatic real-world generalization gap: accuracy fell to 31.4% when the model was tested on independently sourced online images taken under different conditions than the training set (vs. the 99.35% in-distribution figure) — this is the single most load-bearing caveat for anyone citing PlantVillage-trained accuracy numbers — [arXiv:1604.03169](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.03169)
- A Kaggle-hosted derivative, "New Plant Diseases Dataset," recreates PlantVillage via offline data augmentation to ~87,000 RGB images across 38 classes (healthy + diseased, by species and disease), split 80/20 train/validation with a separate 33-image test folder — [Papers with Code: New Plant Diseases Dataset](https://paperswithcode.com/dataset/new-plant-diseases-dataset); [hyper.ai dataset listing](https://hyper.ai/en/datasets/43960)
- PlantDoc (Singh, Jain, Jain, Kayal, Kumawat, Batra — IIT Gandhinagar; presented at CoDS-COMAD 2020) was built explicitly to address the lab/field gap and India's stated 35% annual crop-yield loss to plant disease; it contains 2,598 data points (per the paper) across 13 plant species and up to 17 disease classes, scraped from the internet (non-lab images) with ~300 person-hours of annotation — [arXiv:1911.10317](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.10317); [PlantDoc GitHub](https://github.com/pratikkayal/PlantDoc-Dataset)
- The PlantDoc authors report that training/fine-tuning on their dataset can increase classification accuracy by up to 31% (relative to models not exposed to non-lab images), and they benchmarked both detection models (MobileNet, Faster-RCNN) and classification models (VGG16, InceptionV3, InceptionResNetV2) — [arXiv:1911.10317](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.10317); [Roboflow PlantDoc summary](https://public.roboflow.com/object-detection/plantdoc)
- A Roboflow-hosted mirror of PlantDoc describes the dataset slightly differently (2,569 images, 30 classes including healthy) than the original paper (2,598 data points, 17 disease classes) — this is a noted discrepancy; the paper and original GitHub repo are the more authoritative source — [Roboflow PlantDoc](https://public.roboflow.com/object-detection/plantdoc)
- The Plant Pathology 2020 Kaggle/FGVC7 challenge used ~3,561 apple leaf images across 4 categories (apple scab, cedar apple rust, "complex," healthy); top three leaderboard entries scored multi-class ROC-AUC of 0.98445, 0.98182, and 0.98089 — [arXiv:2004.11958](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.11958); [PMC7526434](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7526434/)
- The successor Plant Pathology 2021 / FGVC8 Kaggle challenge expanded to ~23,000 expert-annotated apple foliar-disease RGB images across 6 labels (healthy, complex, rust, frog-eye leaf spot, powdery mildew, scab — note: an image can carry multiple labels), with varied backgrounds/maturity/lighting to better reflect field conditions — [arXiv:2310.12509](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12509); [HuggingFace: timm/plant-pathology-2021](https://huggingface.co/datasets/timm/plant-pathology-2021)
- A 2026 paper evaluating out-of-distribution detection methods notes that the Plant Pathology 2021 dataset has class imbalance and limited total size, and states the underlying classification task is "largely considered solved" on this specific benchmark — a caution against over-indexing on it as a frontier benchmark — [arXiv:2605.08618](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08618)
- A 2026 review paper (on grape leaf disease specifically) argues PlantVillage-style lab-background accuracy "carries limited information about behavior on imagery acquired under a different pathogen profile and a different acquisition protocol," and a related FieldPlant paper is cited as finding that PlantVillage-trained models have "very low accuracies" on field images with complex backgrounds and multiple leaves per image — [arXiv:2608.20608](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.20608)
- India-specific datasets exist but are narrower/less standardized than PlantVillage: the Indian Rice Disease Dataset (IRDD) — rice leaf images from fields in West Bengal, smartphone-captured under varied lighting, 2 classes (BrownSpot, Healthy), produced by teams at IIIT Kalyani and IIT Kharagpur, DOI 10.21227/4rmf-gd63 — [IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/comment/6166)
- Other India-relevant crop/disease work found: a cotton-leaf CNN study reporting 99.39% test accuracy across 22 leaf disease types on a near-balanced assembled dataset (Springer/CottonLeafNet) and a separate 4-class cotton study (fusarium wilt, curl virus, bacterial blight, healthy) where ResNet50 scored 100% and InceptionV3 98.75% — these are small, single-paper, non-standardized datasets rather than widely adopted public benchmarks — [CottonLeafNet](https://kr.cup.edu.in/handle/32116/3915); [IJICT cotton leaf study](https://ijict.iaescore.com/index.php/IJICT/article/view/21459)
- No wheat-specific Indian public image dataset surfaced in this search pass — flagged as a gap.

### Inferences
- PlantVillage/its Kaggle derivative remains the easiest dataset to prototype with (large, clean, well-labeled, free), but any team using it should explicitly caveat that in-distribution accuracy (often 97–100%, see next section) does not predict real-field performance, per Mohanty et al.'s own 31.4% out-of-distribution figure and the more recent FieldPlant/grape-review critiques.
- For an India-focused product, no single large, standardized, multi-crop Indian public dataset was found comparable in scale to PlantVillage; PlantDoc is the most relevant single dataset (India-authored, motivated by Indian crop-loss statistics, non-lab images) but is small (~2,600 images) and would likely need combining with PlantVillage or additional data collection/augmentation for production use.
- Given the stated "accuracy saturation" and "task largely solved" caveats for both PlantVillage and Plant Pathology 2021, a team should treat leaderboard accuracy on these benchmarks as a floor demonstrating basic model competence, not evidence of field readiness.

### Gaps
- Could not verify the exact current license terms for the Kaggle "New Plant Diseases Dataset" derivative (one source lists "Other" as license) — should be checked directly on Kaggle before commercial use.
- No wheat-specific or multi-crop (rice+wheat+cotton combined) large Indian public dataset was located; coverage found was single-crop (rice-only, cotton-only) and from scattered, non-standardized sources (IEEE DataPort, Mendeley, individual papers).
- Could not locate/verify the original Hughes & Salathé 2015 PlantVillage dataset-creation paper itself (as distinct from the 2016 Mohanty et al. deep-learning application paper) within this search pass, so the dataset's own provenance paper is not separately cited here.

## What model architectures are commonly used/published for this task, and what accuracy do they report on benchmarks like PlantVillage?

### Takeaway
CNN transfer learning (ResNet, EfficientNet, VGG, MobileNet, Inception family) remains the dominant published approach and routinely reports 97–100% accuracy on PlantVillage-style benchmarks; vision transformers (ViT and variants: Swin, DeiT, MobileViT, custom "Tiny-ViT"/"Multi-ViT" designs) have increasingly matched or slightly exceeded CNNs on the same benchmarks (also typically 98–99.9%), but several more recent 2025–2026 papers explicitly show both architecture families degrade sharply on field-realistic or from-scratch/non-pretrained settings, reinforcing that these high numbers are benchmark-specific rather than general real-world accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- Mohanty, Hughes & Salathé (2016) — the foundational "CNN transfer learning on PlantVillage" paper — reported 99.35% in-distribution accuracy, 31.4% out-of-distribution accuracy (see dataset section above for full citation) — [arXiv:1604.03169](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.03169)
- A review/benchmark source cites EfficientNetB0 reaching 99.56% accuracy across PlantVillage's 38 classes, and cites an ensemble of deep architectures reaching 99.89% on the same benchmark — [via arXiv:2608.20608 search summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.20608)
- A Papers-with-Code-style leaderboard (hyper.ai mirror) lists µ²Net+ (ViT-L/16) at 99.89% and a leaf-disease-classification SOTA entry at 98.87% accuracy — [hyper.ai PlantVillage leaderboard](https://vercel.hyper.ai/en/sota/tasks/image-classification/benchmark/image-classification-on-plantvillage)
- A tomato-only 10-class PlantVillage subset study found MobileNetV2 achieved the highest accuracy among compared models at 97.26% on an independent test set, while a classical ML baseline (KNN) achieved only 58.22% — illustrating the gap between modern CNN transfer learning and non-deep-learning baselines — (source: search-aggregated from the "PlantVillage dataset paper classification accuracy benchmark" query; original paper title not individually captured — **treat as needing primary-source confirmation**)
- Vision transformer results (2025–2026 papers): a "Multi-ViT" attention-score ensemble reported >99% accuracy on apple/grape/tomato leaf datasets, beating ResNet/VGG/MobileNet baselines — [PMC11723448 (Sensors 2025)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11723448/)
- "Tiny-ViT," a compact vision transformer for potato leaf disease, reported 99.85% test accuracy and 99.82% mean cross-validation accuracy, ahead of DeiT-Small, Swin-Tiny, and MobileViT-XS in the same paper — [arXiv:2603.26761](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.26761)
- A ViT evaluated on DiaMOS (a field-collected leaf dataset from Sardinia, not a lab dataset) reported 98.5% precision and 97.83% F1, outperforming fine-tuned InceptionV3, EfficientNetB0, VGG, and ResNet50 in that paper's comparison — (IEEE ICSC 2023, cited via Amrita University publication page) — [Amrita: DiaMOS ViT paper](https://www.amrita.edu/publication/diamos-plant-leaves-disease-classification-using-vision-transform/)
- A fine-tuned pretrained ViT on PlantVillage's 39-class formulation (38 disease/healthy classes + 1 "no background leaf" class) reported 98.22% accuracy — (IEEE 2023, cited via search aggregation — primary paper title/DOI not individually captured in this pass)
- A more sobering 2025 comparison of potato-disease models found ViT-B-16 achieved the highest AUC (0.87–0.89) across folds while CNN baselines had the lowest AUCs in that same study — explicitly framed by the authors as highlighting a gap between academic benchmark performance and practical performance — (arXiv, cited via search aggregation; exact arXiv ID not individually captured in this pass — **flagged for confirmation**)
- A 2026 ICETTACS paper on cinnamon/rice/potato/maize disease classification found a ViT trained from scratch achieved ~84% accuracy (macro F1 0.84), and an ensemble of that ViT with VGG16 reached 89.57% accuracy (macro F1 0.90) — notably lower than the >98% figures common elsewhere, attributed to training from scratch rather than using pretrained weights, and the companion paper notes this ensemble did not beat the strongest pretrained transformer baseline — [Atlantis Press ICETTACS](https://www.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/icettacs-26/126027782)

### Inferences
- The published literature strongly favors transfer learning (pretrained ImageNet weights, whether CNN or ViT backbone) over training from scratch; papers that train from scratch report meaningfully lower accuracy (~84–89%) even on similar tasks, which is directly actionable for a small team with limited compute/data — fine-tuning a pretrained EfficientNet/MobileNet/ResNet or a pretrained ViT/DeiT is the well-established, lower-risk path versus training a custom architecture from zero.
- MobileNet/MobileNetV2/EfficientNet-B0-class architectures are repeatedly cited as strong and notably lightweight choices (relevant for a mobile-first crop-diagnosis app), achieving accuracy competitive with larger architectures (e.g., MobileNetV2's 97.26% beating other compared models in one tomato study) while being cheaper to run on-device or on low-cost inference servers.
- Because PlantVillage-style benchmark accuracy is widely reported as "near-ceiling" / "largely solved" by multiple 2025–2026 papers, a small team should not use PlantVillage accuracy alone to select an architecture; field-realistic benchmarks (DiaMOS, FieldPlant, PlantDoc) and the explicit in-distribution/out-of-distribution accuracy gap are better indicators of what will hold up in production.

### Gaps
- Several accuracy figures above (the tomato/MobileNetV2 study, the 39-class PlantVillage ViT study, and the "potato disease academic-vs-practical gap" ViT-B-16 study) were captured via search-result aggregation without this session individually opening and confirming the primary paper's title/DOI/arXiv ID — these are flagged inline as needing confirmation and should be re-verified against the primary source before being treated as fully citable in a final report.
- Could not locate a single, recent (2025–2026), large-scale, multi-architecture benchmarking paper that directly compares CNN vs. ViT families on the same field-realistic (non-PlantVillage) dataset under identical training conditions — the comparisons found are each from different papers with different protocols, so cross-paper architecture comparisons should be treated as indicative, not rigorous head-to-head results.

## Vision-capable multimodal LLM APIs used in production for this use case, and free/low-cost options for small teams

### Takeaway
No source found in this research pass documents a named production crop-disease-diagnosis deployment built on a general-purpose multimodal LLM (e.g., GPT-4V/GPT-4o, Gemini, Claude) rather than a purpose-built classifier/API — the identified production players (Plantix, Plant.id/Kindwise, PlantNet) all use custom-trained classification models, not general vision-LLMs. For a small team on a minimal budget, OpenRouter remains the most accessible route to free vision-capable model inference, but this session could not fully confirm the current (October 2026) list of specific free vision model IDs due to tool/network limitations.

### Cited Findings
- OpenRouter has (as of a Feb 1, 2026 release noted in search results) a "Free Models Router" (`openrouter/free`) that accepts both text and image input and returns text, selecting at random among OpenRouter's free models — meaning image input is available through this routed endpoint even without picking a specific model — (source: search-aggregated summary; primary OpenRouter page not directly fetched due to a tool network error — **flagged for confirmation**)
- OpenRouter's stated free-tier (`:free` suffix) rate limits are 20 requests/minute always, with daily caps of 50 requests/day with no credit purchase or 1,000 requests/day once $10+ in credits has been purchased — (source: search-aggregated from third-party "OpenRouter Free Models List 2026" guides; not independently confirmed against OpenRouter's own docs in this pass due to a WebFetch DNS failure — **flagged for confirmation**)
- A third-party model-capability reference (dated 2026-04-30) lists `google/gemma-3-27b-it:free` as a free, vision-capable model available via OpenRouter — [Model Capabilities Reference](https://czmissioncontrol.duckdns.org/skills/creative/custom-ai-chat-ui/references/model-capabilities.md) (third-party, unofficial source; flagged as lower confidence)
- Free Qwen vision-language models (e.g., `qwen2.5-vl-32b-instruct:free`) are documented as accepting text+image input on at least one other LLM gateway (LangDB), suggesting Qwen-VL free variants exist in the broader free-tier ecosystem, though this specific listing was not confirmed as currently live on OpenRouter itself — [LangDB Qwen2.5-VL-32B listing](https://langdb.ai/app/providers/openrouter/qwen2.5-vl-32b-instruct:free)
- This session's attempts to directly fetch OpenRouter's own models-listing page (to confirm the live, current free+vision-capable model set) failed with a DNS resolution error (`getaddrinfo ENOTFOUND openrouter.ai`) — this is an environment/tool limitation in this research session, not a finding about OpenRouter itself.

### Inferences
- The absence of any found production case study using a general multimodal LLM (GPT-4o/Gemini/Claude-class) for crop disease diagnosis, contrasted with multiple confirmed production deployments of purpose-built classifiers (Plantix's custom deep-learning models, Kindwise's crop.health/plant.health classifiers, PlantNet's species classifier), suggests the current (as of this research) industry default for this specific use case is still purpose-trained CNN/ViT classifiers rather than general vision-LLMs — likely because of cost-per-call, latency, and the benefit of narrow, well-calibrated class probabilities (important for an agricultural diagnosis tool) versus a general LLM's open-ended, harder-to-calibrate output.
- For a small/student team, a practical low-cost architecture implied by this research is: use a free-tier general vision-LLM (via OpenRouter or similar) as a flexible prototype/fallback or for natural-language explanation of a diagnosis, while relying on a fine-tuned lightweight CNN (MobileNet/EfficientNet-B0 class) trained on PlantVillage + PlantDoc + any Indian-specific data collected, as the primary classifier — consistent with the architecture findings above.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm, with a primary-source fetch, the complete and currently accurate (October 2026) list of free, vision-capable models on OpenRouter — this is the single most time-sensitive, fast-moving fact in this report and should be re-checked directly at openrouter.ai/models (filtered by modality=vision and free pricing) before any product decision is finalized, since this session's WebFetch tool could not reach openrouter.ai (DNS resolution failure) and WebSearch results for this question were inconsistent/third-party-sourced.
- No evidence (confirmed or denied) was found regarding whether Google Gemini or OpenAI GPT-4o/GPT-5-class vision models currently offer a no-cost free tier usable for an app's production inference (as opposed to one-off/limited playground use) — not confirmed in this pass.
- No case studies or technical write-ups were found describing a production agricultural app that uses a general multimodal LLM API as its primary (not supplementary) diagnosis engine — this absence itself is noted above as a finding, but it is an absence-of-evidence, not proof that no such deployment exists.
